Evidence and accuracy

Fact-Checking Policy

Every consequential statement should be traceable to a source strong enough, current enough, and relevant enough to support the words on the page.

Fact-checking asks a simple but demanding question: can a reader trace a statement back to evidence that actually supports it? On a health site, this means checking more than spelling and dates. The strength, age, relevance, and limitations of a source can change what a claim is allowed to say.

Evidence-aware does not mean evidence-perfect

Research develops over time, and reputable sources can disagree. Our standard is to represent the best available support accurately, state meaningful uncertainty, and correct the record when stronger information changes the picture.

What we check

Checkable material includes definitions, descriptions of symptoms or risks, statements about benefits and harms, numerical claims, dates, quotations, product details, and summaries of guidelines or studies. Fact-checking also tests the relationship between ideas: an observed association must not be rewritten as proof that one factor caused another.

Headlines, captions, summaries, and calls to action receive the same scrutiny as body text. A cautious article can still mislead if its headline promises a cure or certainty that the cited research does not provide.

Our source hierarchy

  1. Primary and synthesized research. Randomized controlled trials and systematic reviews in peer-reviewed journals are preferred where they can answer the question.
  2. Clinical and public-health guidance. Current standards from recognized bodies, including the American Diabetes Association and World Health Organization, help establish clinical context.
  3. Reputable medical institutions. Sources such as the National Institutes of Health, Mayo Clinic, and Cleveland Clinic may be used for accessible background when primary sources are unavailable or need explanation.
  4. Relevant expert context. Verified professional input can clarify complex evidence, but opinion should be identified as such and should not replace research.

No hierarchy makes a source automatically correct. A fact-checker considers whether the source is current, whether the study population resembles the group discussed, whether the outcome matches the claim, and whether later evidence has changed the conclusion.

How a claim is checked

  1. Isolate the claim. Break broad statements into specific points that can be supported or challenged.
  2. Open the original source. Whenever practical, inspect the guideline or study rather than relying on a press report or search excerpt.
  3. Test the match. Confirm that the source addresses the same population, intervention or exposure, comparison, and outcome described in the article.
  4. Read beyond the conclusion. Note limitations, uncertainty, study design, and whether the finding has independent support.
  5. Revise to the evidence. Narrow, qualify, replace, or remove language that the source cannot sustain.

Sources and practices we do not treat as proof

Anecdotes, testimonials, and personal success stories cannot establish a medical effect. Press releases are designed to announce findings, not to replace the underlying paper. A commercial study may be relevant, but it should not be presented without disclosure and independent corroboration where a conflict could affect interpretation.

We do not support claims by stacking multiple low-quality sources, citing a source that merely repeats another publication, or linking to a homepage that does not document the statement. A citation should sit close enough to the claim for readers to understand what it supports.

Our standards prohibit unsupported claims that supplements, diets, or lifestyle interventions cure or reverse diabetes, and they prohibit advice to stop prescribed medication without first consulting a clinician. Product marketing language is not evidence of safety or effectiveness.

Fact-checking and medical review

Fact-checking and medical review overlap, but they answer different questions. A fact-checker asks whether details and citations are accurate. A medical reviewer evaluates clinical meaning, safety, guideline alignment, and whether the article could be misread as individual advice. Content with clinical claims should pass both relevant checks.

An author remains responsible for faithful sourcing, and an editor remains responsible for the whole page. No badge or workflow removes the need for readers to consult qualified professionals about personal care.

Commercial content receives the same standard

Natural Health One may earn revenue from display advertising and affiliate links. A potential commission does not lower the evidence threshold or allow a brand to choose the conclusion. Affiliate relationships should be disclosed, and product assessments should use independent research, relevant expert input, and objective criteria.

Manufacturer specifications can establish what a product claims to contain or do, but they do not independently establish a health benefit. Reviews are revisited when a significant product version or study changes the facts readers need.

Updates, corrections, and reader input

Sources can be updated, corrected, retracted, or overtaken by new guidance. High-priority clinical content is reviewed every 12 months or sooner after major guideline changes; lifestyle and nutrition guides every 18 to 24 months; and device or product reviews after a significant version or study appears.

If an error is substantiated, we correct it promptly. Significant corrections should state what changed and why. To report a concern, contact the editorial team with the page, disputed wording, and a reliable supporting source if available. We review content reports, but we cannot answer personal diagnostic or treatment questions.
