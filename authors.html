Our Authors

Good health writing earns trust through traceable evidence, clear scope, and honest attribution—not through impressive-sounding biographies.

Authors turn medical literature, clinical guidance, and practical questions into understandable articles. Their job is not to diagnose readers or replace individualized care. It is to explain what reliable sources say, identify uncertainty, and make clear when a decision belongs with a qualified healthcare professional.

This deliberate gap is more honest than displaying placeholder identities, implied qualifications, or biographies that readers cannot confirm.

What an author is responsible for

An author is accountable for the structure and initial accuracy of a piece. That includes defining the reader’s question, keeping the article within scope, finding suitable sources, and distinguishing established guidance from emerging or limited evidence. Authors should use plain English without flattening important clinical distinctions.

Our editorial policy describes contributors with relevant expertise, including medical writers, health journalists, registered dietitians, and diabetes educators. The expertise needed depends on the assignment. A nutrition guide and an explanation of a glucose-monitoring device may require different experience, source material, and clinical review.

How authors work

  1. Define the question. The draft should answer a specific reader need rather than chase an alarmist headline.
  2. Build from credible evidence. Priority goes to peer-reviewed research, systematic reviews, controlled trials, and current guidance from recognized clinical and public-health bodies.
  3. Write with proportion. Benefits, limitations, risks, and areas of disagreement should receive context. Association should not be described as causation.
  4. Submit to checks. Factual claims are checked, and content with clinical claims follows the medical review process before publication.
  5. Respond to updates. Authors and editors may revise a page when evidence, guidance, products, or material facts change.

What a verified profile should tell you

A useful profile is evidence about authorship, not decoration. Before Natural Health One publishes an individual’s name, credentials, or professional biography, those details must be verified. A completed profile should make the person’s role, relevant subject experience, and any qualifications claimed on the site understandable to a reader.

We will not infer a title from someone’s writing experience, elevate a short course into a clinical credential, or label a contributor as a doctor, dietitian, educator, or other regulated professional without verification. Where a credential has a scope or jurisdiction, the profile should not imply broader authority. If profile information cannot be confirmed, it will remain unpublished.

Authors, reviewers, and fact-checkers are different roles

Authorship does not itself prove medical review. A medical reviewer assesses clinical accuracy, alignment with current guidance, nuance, and safety language. A fact-checker verifies whether sources support specific statements and whether names, dates, quotations, and other checkable details are represented correctly. An editor considers clarity, structure, tone, sourcing, and compliance with site standards.

One person may have experience relevant to more than one task, but the functions should not be blurred for the reader. When attribution is available on an article, the author and medical reviewer should be identified according to the work each actually performed.

Independence and conflicts

Authors must not allow advertisers, affiliate partners, brands, or manufacturers to dictate conclusions. Natural Health One may use display advertising and affiliate links, but commercial relationships are separate from editorial decision-making. A financial relationship relevant to a page should be disclosed clearly.

Writers should not rely on testimonials as proof, present a press release as independent research, or repeat a commercial study without appropriate scrutiny and corroboration. Product coverage must not promise that a device, supplement, diet, or service will prevent, treat, cure, or reverse diabetes beyond what robust evidence supports.

Accountability after publication

An author byline does not make an article permanent or infallible. New guidelines can change the appropriate framing, a source can be withdrawn, or a reader can identify an error. We review high-priority clinical material on a shorter cycle and can update sooner when important evidence changes.

Readers should evaluate an article by its sources, dates, disclosures, review information, and language—not by a title alone. If a claim appears unsupported or an attribution seems inaccurate, contact the editorial team. Significant corrections should explain what changed and why.
