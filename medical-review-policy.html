Medical Review Policy

Clinical review is a documented checkpoint: the evidence must support the claim, the context must fit, and general education must never masquerade as individual care.

Natural Health One’s medical review process is designed for content that makes clinical or medical claims. Its purpose is to test accuracy, context, safety, and alignment with current guidance before publication and when meaningful changes require a new review.

Review is not personal medical advice

A reviewed article remains general education. It cannot account for a reader’s diagnosis, medications, laboratory results, pregnancy status, other conditions, or treatment goals. Never delay care or change a prescribed treatment because of website content.

Which content requires review

Articles that discuss diagnosis, disease mechanisms, treatment, medication, clinical targets, risks, complications, or other medically consequential claims fall within the review process. Nutrition, exercise, supplements, monitoring tools, and devices can also require review when they include safety guidance or claims about health effects.

Not every sentence carries the same risk. A definition may need confirmation against a guideline, while a treatment comparison may require deeper review of trials, contraindications, and uncertainty. Editors should match the depth of review to the potential consequence of getting the claim wrong.

Reviewer selection and verification

Clinical content is assigned to a reviewer with expertise relevant to the subject. Our editorial policy identifies physicians, specialists, and registered dietitians among the professionals who may be appropriate for different topics. The reviewer’s role is separate from the author’s role, even when both understand the subject.

Natural Health One will publish a reviewer’s name and professional claims only after verification. Until individual profiles and credentials are confirmed, the Medical Reviewers page explains responsibilities without presenting an unverified roster. We do not use honorary titles, composite identities, or placeholder biographies.

The review process

  1. Editorial preparation. The draft identifies its purpose, intended audience, principal claims, sources, and any areas where evidence is uncertain.
  2. Clinical assessment. The reviewer checks medical statements, source quality, guideline alignment, terminology, risk framing, and whether conclusions exceed the evidence.
  3. Safety and scope check. Language that could be mistaken for a diagnosis or an individualized treatment recommendation is revised. Relevant cautions are added where omission could mislead.
  4. Revision. The author or editor addresses questions, replaces weak sources, narrows claims, or removes unsupported material.
  5. Resolution and publication. Clinical concerns must be resolved before the material is approved for publication. Review information should be shown with the article when available.

What reviewers look for

Reviewers assess whether the article distinguishes causation from association, describes evidence in proportion to its strength, and avoids implying that one result applies to everyone. Numerical targets and clinical recommendations need particular care because they can vary with personal circumstances and change as standards evolve.

Sources should favor peer-reviewed primary research, systematic reviews, randomized controlled trials, and current clinical guidelines. Recognized medical institutions may provide explanatory context. Testimonials, anecdotes, and press releases are not medical evidence. Commercial research should not stand alone when independent corroboration is needed.

Reviewers also look for language that overpromises. Natural Health One does not permit claims that a supplement, diet, or lifestyle intervention can cure or reverse diabetes without robust clinical evidence, or content that encourages readers to stop prescribed medication without speaking with a clinician.

Review cycles and triggers

High-priority clinical pages, including content about medication, A1C targets, and treatment protocols, are scheduled for review every 12 months or sooner when major guidelines change. Lifestyle and nutrition guides are scheduled every 18 to 24 months. Product and device reviews are revisited when a significant new version or study appears.

A calendar is only a backstop. Safety alerts, withdrawn research, major evidence, new guidance, or a substantiated reader concern can trigger earlier assessment. A minor copy edit does not necessarily require a full medical rereview; a change to a clinical conclusion does.

Independence and disagreement

Advertisers, affiliate partners, and product manufacturers do not approve medical conclusions. Reviewers should assess the evidence rather than protect a commercial relationship. If a financial connection is relevant to a page, it should be disclosed.

Reasonable experts can interpret limited evidence differently. When uncertainty matters, the article should say so rather than manufacture consensus. If an author and reviewer cannot resolve a material clinical concern, the disputed claim should not be published as settled fact.

Corrections and reader reports

Review cannot eliminate every error. When a factual or clinical problem is identified, the team should investigate the original sources, determine the scope of the issue, and correct it promptly. Significant changes should include a clear note describing what changed and why.

To flag a concern, use the contact page and include the article, passage, and supporting source if possible. Reports about site content are welcome; requests for diagnosis or treatment must be directed to a qualified healthcare professional.
